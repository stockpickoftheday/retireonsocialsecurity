Break-even ages · When waiting catches up

Social Security Break Even Age: When Waiting Pays Off

The break-even age is when waiting to claim catches up with claiming early, in total dollars collected. For someone whose full retirement age is 67: claiming at 67 instead of 62 breaks even around age 78 or 79; claiming at 70 instead of 62 breaks even around age 80 or 81; and 70 instead of 67 around age 82 or 83. Live past the break-even age and waiting paid off. Die before it and claiming early was the better call. It's just arithmetic. Your health and family history are what make the decision personal.

What "break even" means

Claiming early gives you more checks, each smaller. Claiming late gives you fewer checks, each bigger. Add up the dollars, and there is one age where the two totals are exactly equal. That is the break-even age. Before it, the early claimer is ahead. After it, the late claimer pulls ahead — and stays ahead for the rest of their life.

These ages barely move with the size of your benefit — they come from the percentages, not the dollars. Use the calculator to get your exact break-even ages for your own benefit and birth year.

A worked example

Full benefit $2,000 a month at 67. At 62 you get $1,400; at 70 you get $2,480. Claim at 62 and by your 70th birthday you have collected $134,400. Claim at 70 and you have collected nothing yet — but from then on you get $1,080 more every month. Divide the $134,400 head start by $1,080 a month and it takes about 124 months, or 10 years and 4 months, to catch up: age 80 and 4 months.

What break-even does not capture

  • Your spouse. If you are the higher earner, your check becomes your spouse's survivor benefit. Waiting can be worth far more to a couple than the break-even age suggests.
  • Needing the money now. Break-even math assumes you can afford to wait. If you cannot, the math does not matter.
  • Peace of mind. A bigger guaranteed check for life is worth something that no total-dollars table can show, especially if you worry about outliving your savings.
  • Taxes and other income. A bigger check can push more of your benefit into tax. See is Social Security taxable?

Break-even ages are arithmetic, not advice. They tell you the age where waiting starts to pay in dollars. Whether you will live past it is a question about your health and your family history — and only you can answer that.
