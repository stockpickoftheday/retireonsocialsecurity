For couples · Spousal and survivor benefits

Social Security Spousal Benefits and Survivor Benefits

If you're married, you may be able to claim a benefit based on your spouse's record instead of your own, up to 50% of their full benefit at their full retirement age. It doesn't reduce what they get. But there's a catch: the spousal top-up never includes delayed retirement credits, so waiting until 70 doesn't raise a living spouse's spousal benefit. The survivor benefit is different. When one spouse dies, the survivor steps into 100% of whatever the deceased was actually receiving, including every dollar of delayed credits. That's why the higher earner's claiming age matters so much for couples.

Spousal benefits: the basics

  • Up to 50% of your spouse's full benefit (their PIA), if you claim at your own full retirement age.
  • You must be at least 62, married for at least one year, and your spouse must already be collecting their own benefit.
  • Social Security pays the higher of the two: your own earned benefit, or the spousal benefit. You do not get both added together.
  • Claiming early cuts it. At 62, the spousal benefit drops to as little as 32.5% of your spouse's full benefit.
  • No delayed credits. Waiting past your full retirement age does not raise a spousal benefit. It tops out at 50%.
  • It costs your spouse nothing. Your spousal benefit does not reduce their check by a dollar.

Example: your spouse's full benefit is $2,400 a month. At your full retirement age you could receive up to $1,200 a month as a spousal benefit — if that is more than your own earned benefit.

Divorced? You may still qualify

If your marriage lasted at least 10 years, you are at least 62, you are currently unmarried, and your ex-spouse is at least 62 and has enough work credits, you can claim a spousal benefit on their record — up to 50% of their full benefit. It does not reduce what they or their current spouse receive.

Survivor benefits: the basics

  • Up to 100% of what the deceased worker was receiving (or was entitled to receive), if you claim at your full retirement age for survivors.
  • You can claim as early as 60 (50 if disabled), at a reduced rate — about 71.5% at 60.
  • Delayed credits count. If the deceased waited until 70, the survivor inherits the full boosted amount, including all delayed retirement credits. This is the biggest reason for a higher earner in a couple to wait.
  • Caring for a young child: a surviving spouse caring for the worker's child under 16 gets 75%, whatever their own age.
  • Marriage length: generally at least nine months before the death (10 years for a divorced survivor).
  • Remarriage: remarrying before 60 usually ends survivor benefits on the deceased spouse's record; remarrying at 60 or later does not.

Why couples should decide together

For a couple, the claiming decision is really two decisions: the lower earner can often claim earlier, while the higher earner waiting until 70 buys the largest possible survivor benefit — a check the surviving spouse may live on for many years. Run both numbers on the calculator and compare the break-even ages together.

Spousal and survivor rules have many exceptions — government pensions, caring for children, and remarriage all change the answer. Confirm your situation with the Social Security Administration at ssa.gov or by calling 1-800-772-1213.

Common questions

How much is the spousal benefit?

Up to 50% of your spouse's full benefit, if you claim at your own full retirement age. It does not reduce what your spouse gets.

What is the survivor benefit?

Up to 100% of what the deceased worker was receiving, if you claim at your full retirement age for survivors. You can claim as early as 60.

Do delayed credits count for survivor benefits?

Yes. If the deceased waited until 70, the survivor inherits the full boosted amount, including all delayed retirement credits.

Can I claim on an ex-spouse's record?

Yes, if the marriage lasted at least 10 years, you are at least 62, currently unmarried, and your ex-spouse is at least 62 with enough work credits.
