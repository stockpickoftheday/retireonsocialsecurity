Full retirement age · The age that sets your benefit

Full Retirement Age: What It Is and When You Reach It

Your full retirement age is the age Social Security pays you 100% of your calculated benefit. It's 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later, and 66 and some months for people born between 1943 and 1959. Claim before it and your check is permanently reduced. Wait past it and it grows 8% a year until 70. It matters for more than the amount: once you reach it, there's no limit on how much you can earn while collecting, and any benefits withheld earlier for working get credited back.

One quirk: if you were born on January 1, Social Security treats you as born in the previous year, so use that year's row. Your exact full retirement age is also on your statement at ssa.gov/myaccount.

Why it matters

  • It sets your full benefit. Your calculated benefit (your PIA) is what you get if you claim exactly at full retirement age.
  • It sets the size of the early cut. The further full retirement age is from 62, the bigger the reduction for claiming at 62 — 30% for anyone born in 1960 or later.
  • It sets the delayed credits. You earn 8% a year only for months after full retirement age, up to 70. With a full retirement age of 67, that is three years and a 24% bonus.
  • It ends the earnings limit. Before full retirement age, earning above $24,480 in 2026 means some benefits are withheld. From the month you reach full retirement age, there is no limit.

Full retirement age is not Medicare age

Medicare starts at 65, whatever your full retirement age is. You can sign up for Medicare at 65 and still wait until 67 or 70 to claim Social Security — the two are separate decisions.

Congress raised the full retirement age from 65 to 67 in 1983, in steps by birth year. The rise is now complete: everyone born in 1960 or later has a full retirement age of 67. Figures verified November 2026.
