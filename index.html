Social Security Calculator: How Much Will You Get?

A Social Security calculator estimates your monthly benefit before you claim it. You enter what you earn, or the benefit from your Social Security statement, and it shows what you'd get at every age you could start, from 62 to 70. It uses the same formula Social Security uses: your 35 highest-earning years, averaged into a monthly figure, then adjusted for when you claim. The number is an estimate in today's dollars. Your real benefit depends on your actual earnings record, which you can check at ssa.gov.

How much Social Security will I get?

How much you'll get depends on two things: what you earned over your working life, and the age you start collecting. Social Security averages your 35 highest-earning years, runs them through its formula, and that gives your full benefit at age 67, for anyone born in 1960 or later. The average retired worker gets about $2,071 a month in 2026. The most anyone can get is $5,181 a month, and only by earning at the top for 35 years and waiting until 70. Your own statement at ssa.gov shows your exact estimate.

Should I take it at 62 or 70?

Claiming at 62 means 70% of your full benefit, for life. Waiting until 70 means 124% — but you skip eight years of checks. The two roughly even out around age 80 or 81: live past that and waiting wins. See the full comparison →

Spousal benefits

If you are married, you may be able to claim up to 50% of your spouse's full benefit — and it does not reduce what they get. The survivor benefit is different: if your spouse dies, you can step into 100% of what they were receiving. Learn how spousal and survivor benefits work →

How the number is worked out

Your 35 best years

Social Security takes your earnings from your 35 highest-earning years, adjusts each year for wage growth, and averages them into a monthly figure called your AIME (average indexed monthly earnings). Years you earned little or nothing still count if you worked fewer than 35 years — they go in as zeros and pull the average down. That is why a long career matters so much.

The 90/32/15% formula

Your AIME runs through a three-part formula to give your PIA (primary insurance amount) — the benefit you would get at full retirement age. For workers first eligible in 2026, the formula pays 90% of the first $1,286 of AIME, 32% of the part from $1,286 to $7,749, and 15% of anything above $7,749. The formula is progressive on purpose: lower earners get a bigger share of their past earnings replaced. The dollar cutoffs, called bend points, rise each year with average wages — for 2027 they are $1,340 and $8,077.

Claiming age: the big lever

Your PIA is only the starting point. Claim before full retirement age and it is cut: 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months early, then 5/12 of 1% per month. With a full retirement age of 67, claiming at 62 means a 30% cut — you get 70% of your full benefit, for life. Wait past full retirement age and it grows 8% a year (2/3 of 1% a month) until 70, when it stops growing. Claiming at 70 gives you 124% of your full benefit.

Why your number may differ slightly

This calculator assumes you earned the amount you typed, in today's dollars, for all 35 years. Your real record is year by year, with wage indexing, and the bend points that apply are from the year you turn 62. That usually moves the answer by only a few dollars. One exception: very high earners. The wage base was much lower in past years, so a flat $184,500 for 35 years overstates the benefit — the real maximum at full retirement age in 2026 is $4,152 a month, not the $4,369 this simple math gives. For an exact figure, check your statement at ssa.gov/myaccount.

Common questions

How much Social Security will I get?

It depends on your 35 highest-earning years and the age you claim. Enter your earnings or the benefit from your statement in the calculator above to see your monthly check at every age from 62 to 70.

Should I take Social Security at 62 or 70?

Claiming at 62 gives you 70% of your full benefit for life; waiting until 70 gives you 124%. The two roughly even out around age 80 or 81, so living past that means waiting paid off.

What is my full retirement age?

It is 67 if you were born in 1960 or later, and 66 and some months if you were born between 1943 and 1959.

Does the calculator send my numbers anywhere?

No. It runs entirely in your browser, and nothing you type leaves your device.
